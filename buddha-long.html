The Life of Gautama Buddha

A detailed retelling of Dr. Vikas Divyakirti’s first lecture on Buddhism—its stories, social setting, central figures, and the differences between traditional biographies and Ambedkar’s account.

1. Before Buddha: Why India was ready for change

Divyakirti begins from a simple principle: philosophers speak to the conditions of their time. The Buddha’s northern India, generally placed around the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, was experiencing enormous social change. Iron tools helped increase cultivation and agricultural output. More food meant greater surplus, trade, towns, and a monetary economy. Historians associate this era with the second urbanization of the subcontinent.

Politically, the region contained powerful mahajanapadas—some monarchies and some clan-based oligarchic republics. Magadha, with rulers Bimbisara and Ajatashatru and centers at Rajagriha and Gaya, would become crucial to Buddha’s mission. Kosala, associated with King Prasenajit and Shravasti, also mattered enormously. Vaishali, Sarnath, and Kushinagar likewise anchor his journeys.

In the Vedic ritual hierarchy, Brahmins held special religious authority. Yet Kshatriya political strength and Vaishya wealth were growing. Divyakirti describes this as status inconsistency: economically or politically powerful groups were not always accorded equal ritual status. Ritual expenses, animal sacrifice, and social ranking also generated criticism. Agriculture’s reliance on animals made nonviolence attractive for practical as well as moral reasons.

Out of this atmosphere emerged the Shramana movements, which questioned inherited authority and sought alternative ways of life. Buddha was not alone. Mahavira advocated disciplined asceticism and rigorous ahimsa. Makkhali Gosala’s Ajivika school stressed niyati, or determinism. Sanjaya Belatthiputta’s skeptical responses to metaphysical questions expressed radical intellectual uncertainty. Others pursued materialist or different karmic accounts.

Divyakirti’s conclusion is that Buddhism was both a spiritual project and a response to deep transformations in social life. Its concern with violence, livelihood, and status could appeal to merchants, rulers, ascetics, and marginalized communities alike.

2. Siddhartha’s family, birth, and the wounded swan

Siddhartha Gautama was born into the Shakya clan near Kapilavastu. His father Shuddhodana was an important Shakya leader, commonly called a king in later narratives. His mother was Mahamaya. After her death, Mahaprajapati Gautami—her sister—helped raise Siddhartha. Figures from this extended family recur later: wife Yashodhara, son Rahula, cousin Ananda, cousin Devadatta, and half-brother Nanda.

Traditional narratives describe Mahamaya dreaming of a white elephant before Siddhartha’s birth at Lumbini. The sage Asita reportedly predicted the child would become either a great ruler or a supreme spiritual teacher. Asita wept, not because of danger to Siddhartha, but because he would not survive to witness his future awakening.

A childhood story sets Siddhartha against Devadatta. Devadatta shoots a swan; Siddhartha removes the arrow and saves it. Devadatta says the bird belongs to the hunter. Siddhartha argues that the claim of the person who preserves a life should prevail over the person who endangers it. Whether understood literally or as moral literature, this episode introduces compassion as a defining virtue.

Siddhartha was raised with comforts and trained for an aristocratic life, but traditional accounts portray him as contemplative and increasingly unwilling to pursue power merely for its own sake. His father tried to surround him with pleasures and divert him from asceticism. Siddhartha married Yashodhara; their son was Rahula. The promised worldly life could not settle his deeper unease.

3. Why did he leave home? Two competing accounts

The four sights in the traditional biography

In Ashvaghosha’s Buddhacharita, Siddhartha is sheltered from troubling realities. When he leaves the palace, he encounters an old man, a sick person, and a funeral procession. His charioteer Channa explains that aging, illness, and death are not unfortunate exceptions but conditions everyone faces. A fourth encounter—with a serene ascetic—suggests another way of living. Siddhartha asks why pleasure and status should matter if both are so impermanent.

He eventually leaves at night with Channa and his horse Kanthaka, changes his appearance, and enters the life of a wandering seeker. This is the Mahabhinishkramana, the Great Renunciation. Divyakirti notes the dramatic and supernatural additions in later retellings and asks how much of this should count as verifiable history.

Ambedkar’s Rohini River conflict

Dr. B. R. Ambedkar objected that a thoughtful adult could hardly have reached his late twenties without ever seeing age or sickness. His The Buddha and His Dhamma instead develops a very different explanation. The Shakya and Koliya communities depended upon the Rohini River for irrigation. Their dispute over water turned violent. Siddhartha opposed the Shakya assembly’s proposal for war and advocated negotiation and a representative commission.

When the assembly persisted and his own position became untenable, he accepted exile rather than take part in what he regarded as an unjust conflict. In this construction, renunciation follows a conflict of conscience about social violence, not merely a private shock over mortality.

Ambedkar’s Yashodhara is also different. Rather than an unsuspecting wife abandoned while asleep, she hears Siddhartha’s reasoning, supports his moral choice, and takes responsibility for their young son. Divyakirti discusses both versions at length because their difference changes the entire interpretation of Buddha’s mission: is suffering mainly an existential problem, or also a political and social one?

4. Teachers, austerities, and a new middle path

Siddhartha’s search brings him to Rajagriha, where King Bimbisara is impressed by him and reportedly offers him worldly opportunity. He refuses. He then studies with Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta, mastering sophisticated forms of meditation. But an elevated mental state is not the same as permanent freedom from suffering: the state passes, while the problem remains.

He undertakes extreme asceticism with five companions, reducing food so radically that traditional representations show him as skeletal. After years of bodily deprivation, he realizes that a severely weakened body cannot sustain clear thinking. He accepts nourishing food from Sujata—traditionally milk-rice—and the five companions abandon him, believing he has lost his discipline.

Yet this reversal is essential. Palace indulgence did not provide liberation; neither did self-torture. The Middle Way rejects both. Buddha’s insight emerges from willingness to revise a failed method rather than defend suffering merely because he has invested years in it.

5. The awakening under the Bodhi tree

Siddhartha meditates at Bodh Gaya. Traditional stories represent the challenge through Mara, whose temptations and threats attempt to break his resolve. After awakening, Siddhartha becomes Buddha, the Awakened One. Divyakirti introduces the philosophical discoveries here but reserves their full examination for subsequent lectures.

The Four Noble Truths are commonly summarized as: suffering or dissatisfaction (dukkha); its arising through causes such as craving (samudaya); the possibility of its cessation (nirodha); and the path leading to that cessation (magga). The Noble Eightfold Path combines understanding, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration.

Pratityasamutpada, dependent origination, is a particularly important idea: experiences and phenomena emerge through conditions. Divyakirti connects this causal approach to Buddha’s distinctiveness as a philosophical teacher. He also explores Ambedkar’s objection to reading the first truth as “everything is nothing but misery,” since ordinary experience plainly includes happiness.

Tradition says Buddha hesitated to teach, doubtful that people could understand something so subtle. A story depicts Brahma persuading him to share it. Divyakirti separates the religious image from the essential point: the private discovery becomes a teaching intended for others.

6. First sermon and the growing Sangha

At the Deer Park in Sarnath, Buddha returns to the five former companions. They initially distrust him because he had abandoned severe austerity. He teaches the Middle Way and the path to ending suffering; they become his first followers. This first sermon is celebrated as Dharmachakra Pravartana—turning the wheel of Dhamma.

Yasa, a wealthy but dissatisfied young man, also becomes a disciple. His father seeks him and encounters Buddha. The episode shows a new religious movement attracting urban households and merchants as well as renunciants. The conversion stories of the three Kashyapa brothers—Uruvela, Nadi, and Gaya Kashyapa—show Buddhist teachings spreading through established communities of ascetics and ritualists.

The two seekers Sariputta and Moggallana previously followed the skeptic Sanjaya. After Sariputta meets the monk Assaji and hears a concise explanation of conditioned arising, he brings Moggallana to Buddha. They become major disciples, providing intellectual and organizational leadership. The Sangha evolves from a small gathering into an influential community.

7. Returning home: Rahula, Upali, and Jetavana

When Buddha returns to Kapilavastu, the reunion with Yashodhara exposes the human cost of departure. His son Rahula asks for his inheritance. Instead of worldly property, Buddha offers him the heritage of the Dhamma, and Rahula enters the monastic community. Nanda, Buddha’s half-brother, illustrates the persistence of attachment even after entering the Sangha: external renunciation does not instantly remove inward desire.

In one especially significant story, several noble Shakya youths and their barber Upali decide to join the Sangha. Upali receives ordination first. Monastic seniority, not aristocratic birth, determines who bows to whom, so the nobles must recognize their former servant as senior. Upali becomes associated with the preservation of the Vinaya—the rules of monastic discipline. Divyakirti regards this as a striking challenge to hereditary social status.

Another crucial supporter is Sudatta, known as Anathapindika, a rich merchant in Shravasti. When he asks if spiritual progress requires abandoning his business, Buddha responds that money itself is not the enemy; being controlled by it and misusing it is. Employees and their families depend on Sudatta’s activities, and he can put wealth to beneficial purposes.

To establish a residence for the community, Sudatta seeks a grove belonging to Prince Jeta. The prince reportedly says he can have it if he covers the ground with gold coins. Sudatta astonishingly begins doing so. The resulting Jetavana becomes one of Buddhism’s central monasteries. Divyakirti connects this idea of responsible wealth with Gandhi’s later notion of trusteeship.

8. Mahaprajapati and women’s spiritual standing

Buddha’s aunt and foster mother, Mahaprajapati Gautami, wants to join the Sangha with a large group of women. Buddha initially refuses. The women persist, shave their heads, and travel as renunciants. Ananda raises the decisive question: are women capable of the same spiritual achievements as men? Buddha concedes that they are. Ananda then asks why the institution should deny them admission.

The women are eventually admitted, though under additional restrictions. Divyakirti refuses to gloss over those unequal rules and the traditional warning that admitting women would shorten the durability of the teaching. The creation of the Bhikkhuni Sangha was consequential, even if it did not produce complete institutional equality.

The Therigatha, early verses attributed to nuns, preserves moving expressions of women’s lives and liberation. Stories involving women across social ranks—including the celebrated courtesan Amrapali—illustrate how the Buddhist path could challenge narrow judgments about respectability and spiritual worth.

9. Devadatta and the temptation of power

Devadatta, the cousin who appears in the wounded-swan story, later enters the Sangha but seeks greater authority. As Buddha grows older, Devadatta demands control over the community. Buddha refuses. Devadatta advocates more severe rules and attempts to draw followers away.

In the traditional account he becomes entangled with Ajatashatru’s rise against his father Bimbisara, and stories describe attempts on Buddha’s life, including assassins and a dangerous elephant. The plots fail. The wider point matters more than the miraculous detail: an institution dedicated to ending craving is still vulnerable to craving for status and control. Buddha’s moral authority cannot simply be seized as an office.

10. The final journey and Mahaparinirvana

By around eighty years old, Buddha has lost senior companions, including Sariputta, and his health is declining. He travels through Vaishali, where the wealthy courtesan Amrapali invites him to a meal. He accepts her invitation despite the interest of Licchavi elites, treating her dignity as independent of conventional social ranking. She later donates her mango grove to the religious community.

At Pava, Chunda offers Buddha a meal. The elderly teacher becomes seriously ill afterward. Buddha is nevertheless anxious that Chunda not be blamed for his death and asks Ananda to reassure him. The precise cause of the illness is uncertain; the story underlines compassion even in bodily pain.

At Kushinagar, Buddha lies between sal trees. Ananda fears for the community’s future and asks whom it will follow. Divyakirti emphasizes Buddha’s refusal to appoint an unquestionable personal authority. The Dhamma and discipline should guide the community. The ideal is often condensed into Appa dīpo bhava—be a light or refuge to yourself.

After his death—Mahaparinirvana—the Mallas organize funeral rites, and multiple communities seek a share of the relics. Relics are divided and stupas erected. Divyakirti observes the irony that a teacher who discouraged attachment ultimately became the center of elaborate veneration.

11. Which Buddha are we reading about?

The traditional biographies include Ashvaghosha’s Buddhacharita and the Lalitavistara Sutra, composed well after Buddha’s lifetime and containing sacred imagery and miraculous elements. Early discourses and monastic traditions were transmitted orally and later collected as the Tripitaka: Vinaya Pitaka (discipline), Sutta Pitaka (discourses), and Abhidhamma Pitaka (systematic philosophical analysis). The gap between the historical Buddha and surviving texts is one reason their stories cannot all be taken as eyewitness history.

Ambedkar’s twentieth-century The Buddha and His Dhamma reconstructs a Buddha concerned with rational consistency, social equality, and human conflict. He questions the four-sights account, crude interpretations of universal suffering, apparent tensions around no-self and rebirth, and the public purpose of a monastic order. His reading became central to Navayana Buddhism, but is not equivalent to older Theravada or Mahayana traditions.

Divyakirti values the tension between these sources. The task is not to flatten legends, canonical discourse, and modern reinterpretation into one supposedly certain history. It is to understand what each account is trying to convey—and what evidence it has.

12. The lecture’s enduring argument

Five threads connect Buddha’s life in this telling. His teaching emerged from a changing society. Human suffering demands inquiry into causes rather than resignation. Liberation requires avoiding destructive extremes. Social dignity need not follow caste, wealth, or gender. And understanding must be cultivated rather than delegated blindly to a teacher or a book.

Do not merely worship the Buddha as a person; try to understand and practice the qualities that made him awakened.

Divyakirti illustrates this through a humorous story about a monk untroubled by the destruction of scriptures or a statue: objects can be replaced, but anger and hatred would destroy the living spirit of Dhamma. He connects this with the Zen provocation about “killing the Buddha” if one meets him—metaphorically, rejecting dependence on authority rather than endorsing violence.

The two renunciation accounts bring everything together. In the traditional version, Siddhartha recognizes the universal fragility of life. In Ambedkar’s version, he also stands against the suffering humans deliberately inflict on one another. Either way, the compelling question becomes not merely “Why do we suffer?” but “What conditions produce suffering, and how can we intelligently reduce them?”
